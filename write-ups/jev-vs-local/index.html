Jev ain't all that.

Jev is great because it’s general. Your project probably needs a few specific decisions, and small local models can make them.

Jev is new. But turning language into a decision isn’t. Jev doesn’t write replies: give it a situation, a question, and possible answers, and it returns a choice and probabilities.

Fantastic, I thought! After checking out some of the things people on the web were using it for, I gave it a shot too. Using my laptop mic, I set up Jev to control my Hyprland desktop. Then I had a realization: I’ve already made this!

Spoiler: Jev won or tied 21 of the 22 tests I ran. It’s good! But I wanted to know how much of that flexibility I actually needed to control my own computer.

Real examples. Real models. The live demos below run models right in your browser. Try your own words in the bottom bar, or tap the mic and say “go to the lineup.” You can cancel model loading and keep reading.

I don’t need an API for every decision.

In 2023 I built a tool picker: embedding-command-search. Give each command a few example phrases, turn them into vectors with BERT, then find the command closest to a new sentence.

I used it to quickly pick which tool to use, in a time long ago when GPT-3.5 Turbo was king. It was fast! It was smooth! But of course, life happened and it got ignored.

For my desktop, the choices are already there: turn the volume up, close a window, open a terminal. I can give a small model examples of those requests and teach it to choose between them. So I dusted off the idea and added one piece: a classifier.

What am I trying to prove?

Can a small local model recognize the same requests, accurately and quickly? For a known set of commands, we can teach it which one you mean.

Try giving this office screen a command in the bottom bar. The map shows where your words land among the training examples.

Try “close the window,” then “do not close the window.” They share almost every word, but only one is a command to act on. That’s where the head helps: examples teach it the difference between similar words and similar intentions.

Your project doesn’t need to solve everything.

Jev is fantastic as a general decision engine. You can change the question and the possible answers without first building a labeled training set for every new task.

Who covers what

No single local method matched Jev everywhere. Together, they equaled or beat it on 21 of 22 tests.

Decider came closest on its own, matching Jev on 15 tests. The other local methods filled in most of the gaps. One even did better at spotting yes/no questions: LLM lower layers got 8/9 against Jev’s 7/9. Reviews with multiple labels were the one test where Jev stayed ahead of every local option.

There’s the useful part: try a few approaches on the decisions your project actually makes. You might only need one of them.

So which one do you need?

Yes, Jev is new, and it’s pretty novel. It’s got people talking, and it’s versatile. But how often does your project actually need a totally general decision model?

For a lot of projects, you already know what you’re trying to decide. That gives you a place to start:

  • Stable labels and some examples? Train a small head on embeddings. It’s tiny and can answer quickly on a laptop.
  • Rules or choices that change every time? Use a general decision model. Decider runs locally; Jev is hosted.
  • Need a written explanation? That’s a job for an LLM.

For my original problem, that little head held up on a plain laptop. It got all nine command questions right, just like Jev, and answered in about 140 ms against Jev’s 243 ms. I can work with that.

That’s my point. Jev makes a lot of different decisions easy to ask for. But with a little planning, a small local model can give you the same answers, often faster, for the job you actually have. You can even ask Claude where to start, then check the suggestion on your own examples.

About the numbers

These are 22 small tests for a personal project, not a general benchmark: two use public banking and restaurant-review data, and twenty are examples I put together. Most have only a handful of questions. They’re enough to explore the idea; your own examples are the next test.

The embeddings head and LLM lower layers learned from labeled examples. Jev and the other methods got the question and possible answers without that training. That preparation is part of the trade-off: I’m happy to teach a model a fixed set of commands, but changing rules and choices call for something more flexible.

Times are typical successful responses after the models are loaded, measured as medians over three passes. Jev includes a network round trip; the local models run on my laptop or GPU workstation. Downloads, startup, and retry waits aren’t included. GPU timings for the head’s fixed-label tests are still unmeasured, so those cells stay blank.
