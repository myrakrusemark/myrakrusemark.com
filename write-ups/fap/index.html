Find a Provider

A health plan's provider search, rebuilt from 2023 to 2026 to show results first and narrow from there, so a search ends in a list instead of a dead end.

RoleUX Team Lead; led the design
TeamContent designers, researchers, designers, accessibility
UsersMedicaid, Medicare and Commercial members
ResultSatisfaction up as much as 40%

The Problem

Find a Provider is where a health plan member looks for a doctor or a pharmacy in their network. When my team took it on, it was more than ten years old. It had grown in small steps for years. Regulatory and contract rules limited so much of what we could change.

The old tool forced members to search narrowly before they saw any results. It asked for a specialty right away, at a specific location, and neither could be changed later without going back and starting all over. More often than not, this yielded zero search results. The filters were there, but changing one would often give zero too. They were spread all over the place, and there was no telling which one might break the entire journey by making you start your search over from the start.

By then we had hit a point where we could call our new UX design system, Fondue, mature. There was always talk about how the old site was looking dated and really needed a refresh. And accessibility issues plagued previous versions of the app, which Fondue was designed to eliminate.

My Part

I was the UX Team Lead. I owned the research, content, design and analytics, and led a team of content designers, researchers and designers. Two other designers and I worked on various parts of the entire tool. I had final approval on the pages and served as the liaison for product owners and deliverables. I also oversaw research and accessibility, planned meetings and directed project decisions.

Getting the work started took a lot of help from leadership. It all started with a trip to Florida, where two big teams met to understand the requirements of such a monumental task. It was successful and informative, but as all things in enterprise go, the project lay around for almost two years until we finally got the green light.

On my part, it was low-key persistence. I didn't push it, but I never let a conversation about issues with Find a Provider go by without mentioning that the UX team had done work to redesign it. I think I was key in keeping the idea in the back of mind for those adjacent to me: UX leadership and the product owners I worked with.

The Florida trip settled on presenting all results first. But the time between that trip and the start of the project let feelings about it go stale, and it took a lot of convincing to keep the idea solid. Leadership didn't want to reopen scope, so I worked it into phase two.

We kept the same rhythm from start to finish:

  • UX team meetings three times a week
  • A weekly session with the external team
  • Regular meetings with Accessibility and Design System partners
  • Every requirement tracked to its status

The project spanned 2023 to 2026. Every screen on the live tool today is mine except the very first one, the page with three cards.

The Evidence

We saw it first as designers, simply trying to get example search results up on the screen just to talk about them. If we were having trouble getting results, our members certainly were. Multiple rounds of research from our research team confirmed it: members were filtering themselves down to zero results and giving up.

From user testing and member interviews, we learned that members wanted to understand that there were actually a lot of providers in their area. They just had to find the right one for them. One finding changed our approach:

Members want to get to the "ocean of data" first, and then dig down to the data or providers that are most important to them.

Seeing that ocean relieved a sense of claustrophobia and isolation that people felt at a time when they least needed that feeling. The old tool worked the other way around.

Before
  1. Pick location, specialty and filters first
  2. Run the search
  3. Often get zero results, and a filter change often gives zero too
  4. Go back and start over to change any setting
After
  1. Type one broad term, like "Urgent Care"
  2. See every match near you, often hundreds, but presented sensibly in a paged format
  3. Filter on the results page, and reset without losing the search
  4. Open a provider's own details page

And when they did get results, the old site's infinite scroll and cramped results caused something closer to agoraphobia. People would give up, having to scroll forever and ever, and they often didn't like having to dig down to the things they found relevant. The map, too, was hidden, and hard to decipher once you got there.

The new design made it much more clear exactly what could be done. It clustered the functions in one area of the experience, where the old version had them spread all over the place.

How It Flows

Members set a location and a plan, then search. They filter on the results page. When a search finds nothing, the page shows them a way back.

  1. 1 · Location and planSet your location and quickly choose your plan from a list, in one step.
  2. 2 · SearchOne search box, with Search by Category and Popular Searches beside it. The plan and location stay on the page, with the ability to change them at any time.
  3. 3 · ResultsFilters sit beside the results. Members can compare up to three providers or switch to a map.
  4. 4 · DetailsEverything about one provider, on its own page.
  5. No resultsA Search Tips page with steps back into the search.

Location and plan come first partly for UX reasons and partly because of tech limits. Coverage, meaning the member's home location and plan, has to be settled before the actual search can run. We simplified the journey by making the home location the default search location, changeable if needed.

The old tool's build-it-all-first problem had a lot to do with not being able to change location and plan later without restarting the whole search. So the new flow was partly results first, then dig, plus easy lateral movement within the data.

An even earlier concept literally dropped members onto a page with all 40-million-some-odd results, and only then let them set these. We determined that crossed the line of balance for good UX.

The plan list shows each plan's contract code, like H1664, because it was a rule.

So to use this tool, you have to know your plan. However, another tool, called "I already have a provider," allowed shoppers to find plans that cover their provider.

Medicaid, Medicare and Commercial members all get the same flow. One huge goal for us was to create a tool that worked for all of these lines of business.

The Search Box

The search page has a single box. It takes a provider's name, a specialty, a facility, a condition, or a provider's ten-digit NPI number.

Not every member understands which field to fill in. They aren't using the site with an employee's mindset, knowing all the possibilities the tool could be used for. They have their very narrow needs. One input should be all that's necessary to search, and it should behave accordingly depending on how you use it.

Which row a member clicks decides what the search means. Search all for "fertility" looks for the word in provider names. The Specialty row looks for reproductive endocrinologists.

The groups in the list were different data types. They could not be mixed together or combined, because the metrics used to rate their relevance were each calculated differently.

Pressing Enter does a text-based search. To choose a search coupled with an actual type, like a group or a specialty, you select an item in the list.

Matching everyday words like "fertility" to specialties was an engineering task, and a monumental one. Many keywords were associated with others using Elasticsearch.

The combo search box behavior I established is still in Fondue, Centene's design system.

Keyboard and screen readers

On the live tool today, focus stays in the box while the arrow keys move through the suggestions, and the list wraps from the last row to the first. Escape closes the list and keeps what was typed. Tab closes it and moves on to the Search button. Enter runs Search all with the typed words. A screen reader reads each row's kind and value together, for example "Specialty, Obstetrics & Gynecology, Reproductive Endocrinology."

Results, Filters and Details

Filters live on the results page. Members set them when they need them and can reset them without losing their search.

The filter rail starts with distance and provider type. Next come features like accepting new patients and virtual visits, then provider tier and board certification. Narrower practitioner and facility filters, such as language, hospital affiliation and accessibility features, open in their own dialogs.

Each result card shows the provider's name and specialties, a short checklist, the address with its distance, and a phone number. Members can check up to three cards to compare, or switch to a map.

Filters sit right next to the results partly to shine light on a previous issue: how do I know what to do after I see results? With so much control, a member needs to understand there are things they can do. Exposing filters right away helps with that.

Five results per page by default was a response to the old site's infinite scroll.

A readable map

We spent a lot of time making mapping clearer in the search results. In the old tool, the map was its own page, reached from a small "(view map)" link on one provider's card, and it showed that one office.

Map View is a button on the results page. The list stays beside the map, a Map Key explains the pins, and each pin shows how many providers match the search at that location. Re-center map brings the view back to the search location.

Many responsive map updates made it easier to use on mobile. On a phone, the results become a row of cards above the map, with dots and arrows to move between them, and the Map Key folds into a button.

Compare, up to three

Compare Providers was a Fondue component this project pioneered. Members can Compare up to three result cards. A tray along the bottom of the page holds them, with Clear All Providers and a Compare button.

The comparison page lines the three up side by side, row by row: the overview checks, address and phone, then specialties, languages, NPI, hospital affiliations, office hours and more. Each one can be removed without starting over.

A page for each provider

Earlier versions fit everything about a provider onto the result card, so members had to decide on incomplete information. The redesign added a Provider Details page with room for credentials, specialties, locations, accepted plans and more.

Card sort testing and member interviews determined what was most important on the details page.

When Nothing Matches

Zero results still happen. When they do, the count line reads "No results found," and a Search Tips panel takes the place of the list. Focus moves to its heading, so keyboard and screen reader users land on the help first. It gives three steps:

  1. Expand the distance to get more results.
  2. Search again using a different or broader key word.
  3. Remove or clear filters to expand your search.

Each step shows a picture of the control it means, and the filters stay on screen beside it, ready to change.

Focus jumps to the heading because we wanted to put these tips in members' faces. Zero results is precisely what we wanted to get members away from, so this state is undesirable. We did what we could to guide people out of it.

Settling the words was a challenging and highly collaborative effort. I worked tirelessly in partnership with our content designer to make sure the words and the design flowed well with each other. We had an equal partnership in design.

Accessibility

We built to WCAG AA on our design system. Regular meetings with the Accessibility and Design System partners kept us from getting too far ahead of them, which saved us rework.

At Centene, our accessibility audits meant reading page source and filling in spreadsheets by hand. Axe kept missing gaps, so I wrote a bookmarklet that draws header structure, tab order and link labels right on the page.

We had an accessibility specialist on our team who would pass through each of our updates and alert us to any accessibility issues. It was very helpful.

Spanish was designed alongside English, by content design.

The design system really kept everything clean. Colors, fonts and basic components were all covered. But we pushed the boundaries a bit with it too. This project pioneered the Compare Providers component. It also premiered the shaded card that says "this is the main thing to focus on before looking at anything else" on the page. And many responsive map updates made the map easier to use on mobile.

Result

  • Satisfaction went up as much as 40%
  • Members start broad and narrow on the results page, instead of rebuilding a search from scratch
  • Zero results come with steps back into the search
  • Every provider has a details page
  • Built to WCAG AA and responsive across devices
  • The combo search box behavior is still in the design system

The tool is live and public: Find a Provider.

The 40% comes from satisfaction studies after launch, compared with the satisfaction studies we ran before the redesign. They were basic UX-side satisfaction studies, powered by interviews, pop-up surveys and UX testing.

Which statuses show on a card was a combination of business requirements and research. But one thing was certain: we didn't want to display two or three red Xs. There are always at least two green checkmarks, whatever those attributes might be. It shouldn't be a ding on the providers themselves. They may be quality; they just don't fit that particular box.

The filters were the same. I wanted to give them more functionality and let members dig down into deeper filters, but research showed that keeping it simple there was best. I always conceded to research in problems like these, because ultimately the tool is for the members, and what they say goes.

The Future

What I'd revisit

Search and filters
I would streamline them. They are a bit noisy. I always pushed back against anyone wanting to put that functionality across the top instead of down the side. I would reconsider the possibility.
Compare
I would spend more time refining how it works: understanding exactly what profile information would be helpful on the compare page, and what could remain hidden.
The combo box
With updated back-end tech, maybe we could improve its UX, remove the groupings, possibly even allow for natural language searches.

Putting the filters down the side was on purpose. I wanted to push something new into the real world, to get real feedback from real people, rather than stick with conventions we already had just because that's what we were used to.

A checker in front of the search

The app allows digging down, back up and to the side. Making that clearer would be of great help: a way to guide people into digging down further, to more refined results.

In my Jev write-up I tested small models that turn a sentence into a choice from a fixed list, with a score for each option. On six banking intents, Jev and my small local head both got 60 of 60. The head answered in about 130 ms on my laptop, against Jev's 262 ms.

The same idea fits here. A fast checker reads what the member typed, picks the kind of care it most likely means, and says how sure it is. When it's sure, the search goes straight to that specialty and says so, with a "Not what you meant?" link. When it isn't, it asks with a few likely choices. "None of these / not sure" is always one of them, because a model that chooses from a fixed list always picks one, even when nothing fits. The checker runs on Enter too, so a typed search gets the same help as a picked suggestion.

Picking "None of these" doesn't have to end the search. It could present further, but still related, types and groups to follow through with.

Conclusion

One huge goal for us was to create a tool that worked for every line of business. Medicaid, Medicare and Commercial members now go through the same flow, and each of them sees every match near them before narrowing anything.

Results first came out of the Florida trip. It took a lot of convincing to keep it solid through almost two years of waiting, but research consistently backed it up: members wanted the ocean first, and then to dig down to the providers most important to them.
