GNAT

An internal tool I designed in 2018 and 2019 that lets new plan teams and product owners set up and change a health plan's websites themselves, with approvals, QA and UAT inside the tool, instead of database changes made by hand and tied to the sprint.

RoleLed the design, with developers on the data structures and IA
TeamExperienced developers; React, with a Spring Boot back end
UsersNew plan teams and product owners; dozens more across the web team
ResultTime from plan requirements to ready for deployment down about 60%

The Problem

At Centene, each of our web apps, member, provider, broker and the rest, was its own Spring Boot monolith. Each one held countless switches that turned sections, functions and actions on or off, plus countless strings that had to be set. All of that meant many database calls across three or four database sources.

When a new plan came online, here is what it took to get its sites set up:

  1. 1 · RequestThe plan has needs, and the regional project manager is told.
  2. 2 · Hand-offThe regional PM tells the new plan's project manager.
  3. 3 · CatalogueThe needs are compared against a catalogue of functions and strings, a menu of sorts.
  4. 4 · ApprovalItems are approved with the regional manager.
  5. 5 · EngineersThe list goes to database engineers, who painstakingly work from Confluence docs and small pages detailing the quirks and exact database operations for each item.
  6. 6 · CodeIssues often turn into code changes, which makes the process longer.
  7. 7 · SprintAll updates are bound to the sprint and deployed alongside other code changes, even though it's mostly database work.
  8. 8 · TestingChanges are checked on an engineer's local, then dev, then test.
  9. 9 · CHGThe database changes are scheduled among other code changes.
  10. 10 · LiveIt finally goes live around midnight on a Thursday, during deployment.

QA and UAT went back and forth, and changes were sometimes delayed many weeks before going live. They weren't code changes. They could have gone live on their own, but they were stuck going through the change management process even when nothing went wrong in dev or test.

My Part

I was one of the people doing this work. For years I stood up these sites by hand, knee deep in the Oracle, Mongo and Postgres databases that all had to work together for millions of members.

I constantly wrote and refined the Confluence pages, trying to keep them from being the bottleneck. It was a nice break from actual environment testing and code work.

Our team's charter was to handle tried-and-true changes that just needed configuration. That often wasn't the case. It took real development and debugging knowledge to solve the problems, so the solution could be handed off to engineers.

I had always worked very closely with product owners when setting up new sites, so what they needed from me was very clear. I basically daydreamed the tool from all the issues I could tell they were running into.

This inspired me to make GNAT.

The name stands for Generalized NPSC Administration Tool. It was named after the WASP tool, a precursor app from a few years before that was just barely related to configurations.

GNAT was my idea. I told my manager on the New Plan Standard Config (NPSC) team at the time. She loved the idea and helped me push it into something real.

Getting there took multiple rounds of service design and meetings. We were setting up a new, internally tested tool connected to test and prod data sources that touched public services. It was a big deal.

I led the design of GNAT. I worked very closely with experienced developers to determine the data structures and information architecture of the tool, and my UX experience was critical to it.

We often talked over sketches. They were in California and I was in Missouri, so we worked over Zoom. I used a lot of Balsamiq and Sketch to show finished designs, but I mainly wanted to make sure the information architecture was there, and they could choose much of the rest. Then I acted as QA for design, putting on my developer hat to add final polish and meet the acceptance criteria.

We were able to test with the people who would use it from early on, and had lots of access to them as we built new features and the early configuration pages.

I was moved off the team at the very end of the project, when I went to UX. I'm thankful that it did get finished and launched.

How GNAT Works

Logged in, new plan team members and product owners use GNAT to set up new plans and their sites, in three steps:

  1. 1 · PlanChoose a plan, or add a new one with its plan ID, name, logo and the rest.
  2. 2 · ConfigureChoose the apps to configure, like member, provider and broker, then work through each app's menu covering every label and configuration.
  3. 3 · ApproveApprovals, QA and UAT happen inside the tool.

Signed out, GNAT also shows a page where anyone can look up a plan's existing configurations. That's a big part of why dozens of people around the web team use it today.

Every plan is a job

A plan was treated as a job, shown either as a new plan or as a change to an existing plan. The jobs list showed where each one stood.

Even with GNAT, we still worked within a strict structure of dev, test and deploy.

A menu for every app

Inside a job, each app got a menu covering every label and configuration. This was the old catalogue, made into the place where the work actually happened. Some configurations were just on or off. Others were strings, like a plan's display name or its member services phone number.

Each setting showed its default, and a plan's own value overrode it.

Complex configurations got their own layout

Some configurations were highly complex, like assessments, and needed their own information architecture.

Approvals at three levels

Jobs showed status and approvals at three levels: individual configurations, apps and plans. QA and UAT confirmed the status and approval of items inside the tool.

The checks were QA. A specialist used tools and manual checking to make sure specific actions could be completed on the site in the test environment. It went back and forth until it was ready for UAT by the product owner, which typically went right through. The approvals really just signaled to the person handling those configurations to update the status of the job, or parts of it.

There was a real undo, and an audit trail. Any configuration could be reversed, in test and in prod.

Over time, trust in the tool grew. That let team members start asking whether some steps, like QA, were still needed at their current intense level, when most of the time UAT could simply approve a change manually. I wasn't on the team by then, but I heard through the grapevine that those discussions were being had.

A Configurable Tool for Configurations

Something that kept coming up was that we were creating a "configurable tool for configurations." GNAT is a dynamic interface for creating and implementing database change templates. By editing the tool's own database and templates, we could do configurations for the databases that controlled the site configurations.

That was an interesting conundrum. It caused some circular thinking that we had to make our way through.

There was some middleware between the React UI and the databases, a Spring Boot application on the back end that automated all of the database work. It was also possible to export the database commands, just in case.

In the end, we were able to design templates for different types of configuration. That won us the ability to configure new configurations as they got developed for our tools. The mindset made further design and development much easier.

The final design was function over form. Just like with Find a Provider, this tool required clarity over aesthetics.

What Changed

GNAT was built to deploy directly to production. The change management process still held updates until the scheduled deployment, so the release gate that remained was policy, not technology.

BeforeWith GNAT
Catalogue of functions and stringsThe menu inside each app in GNAT

Result

  • Time from receiving a plan's requirements to ready for deployment fell by about 60%
  • A plan site could be set up in an afternoon
  • New plan teams and product owners make plan changes themselves, without database changes made by hand
  • Approvals, QA and UAT moved into one tool
  • Any configuration could be reversed, in test and in prod
  • Still in use today by dozens of people around the web team
  • A signed-out page where anyone can look up a plan's existing configurations

We measured the 60% after GNAT had been out and in use for about a year. The savings grew over the next couple of years as GNAT was adopted for more kinds of configuration. Within a couple of years it was used for almost all of them.

Adoption was spotty at first. Interestingly, usage really took off, especially on our UX team, because people would use the signed-out part of the tool just to see what was currently configured within specific plans, since it was very convenient for that. Otherwise, new plan team members and product owners used it for jobs.

Looking back

Honestly, I would have chosen to stay on that team to keep working on the tool, and maybe move to UX a bit later. But I'm happy I designed it, and I'm glad I had the chance to help make our processes more efficient. I've learned more about IA since then, and it helps me make better design decisions, but the tool turned out well nonetheless, so I wouldn't change anything.

What I remember most is seeing a non-expert's face light up when they make the same connections the developer did. The design made the system legible to them, and that is the work I want to keep doing.
